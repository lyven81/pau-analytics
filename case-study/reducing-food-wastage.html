Reducing Food Wastage: What Ingredient Purchase Data From a Malaysian Chinese Restaurant Reveals

Wei Keong runs a sit-down Chinese restaurant in Petaling Jaya. Forty seats, lunch and dinner service, six days a week. He has been in business for nine years. His cooking is well-reviewed, his regulars are loyal, and his revenue has been steady for three years — steadily flat, not steadily growing.

He came to Pau Analytics with a question framed simply: “My food costs keep going up but I am not buying more than before. Where is the money going?”

We asked for his purchase records and, for one month, his kitchen waste log — what was thrown out at the end of each shift, by item and approximate quantity. We analysed six months of purchasing data alongside four weeks of detailed wastage records. The data told a clearer story than he expected.

What the Data Showed

The first finding was the scale. Total measured wastage was equivalent to 22% of food cost. Wei Keong’s monthly food spend was running at approximately RM19,800. Wastage was absorbing roughly RM4,356 of that every month. His food cost ratio was 38% of revenue — the industry benchmark for a similar-format Chinese restaurant in Malaysia sits between 30% and 34%.

The second finding was that wastage was highly concentrated. Four ingredients accounted for 77% of total wastage by value:

  • Whole fish: 31% of wastage. Over-ordered on Mondays based on weekend demand assumptions. Weekday fish consumption was consistently 35% lower than weekends, but the Monday order did not adjust for it. Surplus fish from Tuesday and Wednesday were being discarded at end of service.
  • Bean sprouts: 18%. Ordered daily in fixed quantities. Prep wastage — the unusable root ends and wilted outer layer — ran at 28% of purchased weight every day but had never been costed as a loss. It was treated as part of prep, not part of the food bill.
  • Roast pork: 16%. Evening service consistently left 600–800g per session unsold. Roast pork cannot be re-served the following day, so the remainder went into the bin five to six nights per week.
  • Leafy vegetables: 12%. Two-day shelf life. Orders twice a week did not match the day-of-week demand pattern, which showed Tuesday and Wednesday consistently running 30% lower volume than Thursday through Sunday.

The third finding was the day-of-week pattern. Tuesday and Wednesday had the highest wastage as a percentage of food prepared — consistently 2.1 times higher than Saturday, the busiest day. The cause was structural: Monday orders were sized for projected weekly demand, but the first two days of the week reliably underperformed those projections and had done so for the full six months studied.

What Changed

Pau Analytics recommended three adjustments.

The first was a split fish order. Rather than ordering for the full week on Monday, Wei Keong would order for three days (Monday–Wednesday) on Sunday, review midweek consumption, and place a second order on Wednesday for the Thursday–Saturday period. This added one delivery per week but removed the structural Monday over-order. Fish wastage dropped by 62% in the first six weeks.

The second was a renegotiated bean sprout delivery schedule. Moving from daily delivery to Monday–Wednesday–Friday, in smaller quantities sized to two-day demand, cut prep wastage from 28% of purchased weight to 19% within the same period. The supplier was already making nearby deliveries on those days, so no surcharge applied.

The third was a daily prep guide — a printed sheet posted in the kitchen each morning showing target prep quantities by item, based on reservation count and historical day-of-week multipliers. No software required. A spreadsheet updated each Sunday night took seven minutes to prepare. The kitchen team followed it without pushback because it told them exactly how much to prep rather than leaving them to guess.

Wei Keong made a fourth change on his own: he reduced the roast pork evening portion from 1.2 kg to 900 g per session, with a standing instruction to reorder from the supplier if stock ran low before service ended. In the first month, it ran short three times. Leftover frequency dropped from six nights per week to one.

The Result

After 90 days, wastage as a percentage of food cost fell from 22% to 11%. Monthly food spend dropped from approximately RM19,800 to RM17,400 — a saving of RM2,400 per month. Food cost ratio fell from 38% to 33%, bringing it within the industry benchmark range.

Wei Keong’s comment at the three-month review: “I did not think I was wasteful. I thought I was careful. The numbers said something different.”

The adjustments required no new equipment, no external system, and no additional staff time beyond the seven-minute Sunday prep sheet. The saving came entirely from doing the same things in a slightly different order, based on data that was already available in his purchase records.
