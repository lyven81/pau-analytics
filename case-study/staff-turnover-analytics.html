Staff Turnover Analytics

Mei Ling manages a restaurant group with three outlets in Petaling Jaya. Between the dining rooms, kitchens, and delivery operations she employs 85 people at any given time. She had always known that turnover was high — F&B always is — but she had never measured it properly. Staff would leave, she would recruit, train, and the cycle continued.

Then she sat down and worked out what each departure actually cost. Recruitment ads, working interviews, training time, the overtime her remaining staff absorbed while the gap was open. The number came to roughly RM3,200 per departure. With 23 to 28 staff leaving each year across her three outlets, she was spending over RM70,000 annually on replacing people — before accounting for the service disruptions and the experience walking out the door.

She brought in Pau Analytics to look at two years of HR records: 85 staff members, 47 confirmed departures, with data on each person's department, shift type, entry wage, age group, and how long they lasted before resigning.

What the Data Showed

The first cut was by department. Kitchen staff made up 67% of all early departures — those who left within their first three months. Front-of-house accounted for 24%, and delivery for the remainder. Kitchen turnover was not a surprise, but the concentration of early exits in that department was striking. Most of those departures happened before the staff member had been there long enough to be fully productive.

The second cut was by shift type. Mei Ling's outlets ran three patterns: fixed AM (6am–2pm), fixed PM (2pm–10pm), and split shifts (11am–3pm, then 6pm–10pm). Split shifts existed because lunch and dinner peaks meant she needed coverage at both ends without paying for a full day's labour in between.

The data showed something she had not expected. Staff on split shifts were 2.4 times more likely to leave within six months than staff on either fixed shift. The difference held across departments and across all three outlets. It was not explained by age, entry wage, or tenure — split shift allocation was the clearest predictor of early departure.

The third cut was by entry wage. Staff hired below RM1,600 per month turned over at three times the rate of those hired above RM1,800. The RM200 gap between those two figures was the clearest wage threshold in the data. Below it, most people left. Above it, most stayed past the one-year mark.

What Changed

Mei Ling restructured her kitchen shift allocation first. She reduced split shifts in the kitchen department from 18 per week to 6, covering the gaps with staggered fixed shifts that gave staff a predictable daily schedule. Where split shifts could not be avoided, she paid a RM12 per shift allowance to compensate for the broken day.

She also raised the entry floor for kitchen hires from RM1,500 to RM1,750, accepting that slightly higher payroll was cheaper than the replacement cycle. Front-of-house entry wages were lifted to RM1,650.

The changes did not require more headcount. They required different allocation of the budget she was already spending.

The Result

At the six-month mark, six-month turnover across her three outlets had fallen by 31%. Kitchen early departures dropped from an average of 11 per half-year to fewer than 8. Recruitment costs for the period were RM22,000 lower than the prior six-month cycle.

Mei Ling now runs a quarterly review of her HR numbers. She looks at tenure by department and shift type, flags anyone approaching the three-month mark with no sign of settling, and uses that as a prompt to check in with their team leader. The data does not manage her people for her — it tells her where to look before the resignation letter arrives.
