Are You Staffing the Right Shifts?

Ahmad Fauzi runs two casual dining restaurants in Subang Jaya. For most of the past year, his labour cost had been running at 37% of revenue. He knew the number was high — the industry benchmark for casual dining is 26–28% — but he could not identify where the excess was coming from. His team was not unusually large. His wages were in line with market rates. The payroll total every month looked broadly similar to the month before. He brought in Pau Analytics to find out what the total was hiding.

Pau Analytics reviewed 8 months of scheduling records, hourly transaction data, and payroll records across both outlets — covering more than 14,000 individual shifts and 180,000 customer transactions. The analysis revealed that the problem was not how much Ahmad was paying his staff. It was when he was scheduling them.

What the Data Showed

When scheduling records were matched against hourly transaction volumes, a consistent pattern appeared across both outlets. During weekday afternoons — the 2pm to 5pm window — both restaurants were averaging 4.8 scheduled staff. During those same hours, the outlets were serving an average of 8.6 covers per hour. At a reasonable service standard of one staff member per 15 to 20 covers, optimal coverage for that window was fewer than one person. Ahmad was scheduling nearly five.

The afternoon lull was not a secret. Ahmad knew the restaurants were quieter between lunch and dinner. What the data made explicit was how consistently overstaffed those hours were, and what it was costing. Afternoon overstaffing accounted for approximately RM4,200 per month in wages paid during hours when fewer than a quarter of the staff on the floor were needed. Across both outlets, 38% of total scheduled shift hours were falling in the 2pm–5pm window, while only 11% of daily covers were served during that period.

Friday and Saturday evenings told the opposite story. During the 6pm to 10pm dinner peak, both outlets were serving an average of 41 covers per hour. The average number of scheduled staff during those hours was 5.1. At peak service standards — where table prep, drinks, food delivery, and floor management require closer to one staff per 12–15 covers — the outlets were consistently undercovered by 2 to 3 people. Customer wait times during Friday and Saturday evenings were running between 14 and 18 minutes. In the 8-month review window, 11 customer feedback submissions mentioned slow service or long waits, all concentrated at the Friday and Saturday dinner peak.

The Hidden Cost of Misaligned Shifts

The pattern was invisible in Ahmad's monthly payroll summary because the two errors cancelled each other out. Total headcount looked normal. Total wage cost moved within a narrow range. What the totals could not show was that 38% of his shift hours were covering 11% of his covers — and that his highest-revenue trading window was consistently running short.

The afternoon overstaffing was a direct cost: wages paid for coverage that was not needed. The evening understaffing was a different kind of cost — not a line on the payroll, but a reduction in what the Friday and Saturday shifts could generate. Slower service at peak hours meant tables turned less frequently. It also contributed to the 3-star reviews that mentioned wait times, which were influencing new customer decisions. Ahmad's Friday and Saturday evenings were his best trading window — and they were being undermined by a staffing allocation that had been set by habit rather than by data.

What Changed

Pau Analytics recommended two adjustments. The first was a reduction in weekday afternoon coverage. Both outlets moved to a two-person afternoon skeleton crew — one kitchen, one floor — during the 2pm to 5pm window on Monday through Thursday. Full team coverage resumed from 5pm onwards. Existing full-time staff whose schedules were affected were shifted to morning or early evening slots, where the transaction data showed moderate coverage was consistently useful. No full-time roles were cut.

The second change was the addition of three part-time evening staff per outlet on Fridays and Saturdays, working the 5pm to 11pm shift. The part-time wage cost was RM840 per month across both outlets. The afternoon reduction saved RM4,640 per month. The net payroll impact was a reduction of RM3,800 per month.

The adjustments were implemented over a two-week transition period. Staff schedules were communicated three weeks in advance. No roles were removed — only redistributed.

The Result

Over the two months following the adjustment, labour cost as a percentage of revenue fell from 37% to 27% — bringing Ahmad's restaurants into the standard range for casual dining. Friday and Saturday revenue at both outlets rose 9%, driven by faster table turnover, fewer order errors, and a reduction in peak wait times from 14 minutes to 8 minutes. Customer complaints mentioning wait times dropped to zero in the second month. Total monthly improvement across wages and Friday–Saturday revenue was approximately RM6,100.

Ahmad now tracks staffing versus covers served on a weekly dashboard. When a shift shows more than one staff member per 12 covers for a sustained two-hour block, it flags for review before the next scheduling cycle. An AI assistant supports him in monitoring shift efficiency and identifying in advance when public holidays or school holidays are likely to shift demand patterns at either outlet.
