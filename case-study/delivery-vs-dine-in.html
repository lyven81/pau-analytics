Delivery vs Dine-In: Which Channel Is Really Working for Your F&B Business?

Rashdan runs a nasi lemak restaurant in Petaling Jaya — a forty-seat space that has been open for seven years. In 2023, he listed on GrabFood and added a WhatsApp channel for office bulk orders. Within six months, his delivery revenue was growing faster than dine-in. His GrabFood order average was RM43, compared to RM31 at his counter. Everything looked positive. But his monthly costs were climbing in ways he could not fully account for, and his kitchen team was running behind on some of the busiest dine-in lunches. He brought in Pau Analytics to look at the full picture across twelve months.

Pau Analytics reviewed 18,400 orders from January to December 2024: 11,200 dine-in (61%), 5,600 GrabFood (30%), and 1,600 WhatsApp bulk orders (9%). The analysis covered order values, net revenue by channel after deductions, menu composition, complaint patterns, and time-of-day and day-of-week distribution.

What the Data Showed

The first finding confirmed what Rashdan already knew. GrabFood orders averaged RM43 — 39% higher than the RM31 dine-in average. On the surface, delivery looked like the stronger channel.

The second finding shifted that picture. After GrabFood's platform commission of 30%, the net revenue per GrabFood transaction fell to RM30.10 — 3% below the dine-in average. When packaging costs were included — averaging RM4.80 per delivery order across containers, bags, and insulation inserts for soups and curries — GrabFood net revenue per transaction dropped to RM25.30. For every RM100 of GrabFood revenue on his books, Rashdan was keeping RM59.

The third finding was about the menu. Rashdan had listed 34 items on GrabFood. Six of them — nasi lemak standard set, nasi lemak ayam berempah, nasi lemak daging rendang, mee goreng mamak, fried chicken rice, and economy nasi campur — accounted for 72% of all delivery orders. The remaining 28 items contributed 28% of orders but a disproportionate share of problems. Of all 1-star and 2-star GrabFood reviews received in twelve months, 67% were attached to items outside the top six. These were dishes with longer prep times, higher packaging complexity, or fragile presentation that did not hold up during a 20-minute ride.

The fourth finding was a time pattern. 74% of all GrabFood orders arrived Monday through Friday between 11am and 1:30pm, concentrated around office buildings within a 3km radius. On weekends, GrabFood volume fell sharply — down 61% compared to weekday lunch average — while dine-in traffic peaked. Rashdan had been running the same GrabFood promotions every day of the week, including weekend meal deals that were generating almost no orders while crowding out his dine-in table-turnover messaging.

What Changed

Pau Analytics recommended four targeted adjustments, none of which required new systems or significant investment.

The first change was a menu streamline. The GrabFood listing was cut from 34 items to 12: the six core bestsellers, plus six additional items selected for travel durability, simple packaging, and consistent prep time. Items with high complaint rates or high packaging costs relative to their order frequency were removed.

The second change was a minimum order threshold for WhatsApp bulk orders, set at RM20 per order. Orders below this level had been generating delivery runs that cost more in staff time and packaging than they returned. The minimum reduced the order count slightly but raised average WhatsApp order value from RM37 to RM44.

The third change was promotion scheduling. GrabFood promotions were restricted to Monday through Friday, targeting the 11am–1:30pm lunch window. Weekend social media content was redirected toward dine-in: photos of the physical space, specials available only at the counter, and a weekend family meal package launched in Month 8.

The fourth change was a kitchen sequencing rule: delivery orders received during the lunch rush were batched by pick-up time rather than order-placed time, allowing the kitchen to complete dine-in orders for occupied tables before packaging delivery runs. This eliminated the bottleneck that had been causing delays for seated customers.

The Result

In the three months following the changes, packaging cost per delivery order fell from RM4.80 to RM2.90, as the streamlined menu eliminated the high-cost items. GrabFood's average rating improved from 4.1 to 4.6 stars. Kitchen errors on delivery orders — wrong items, missing components — fell 61%. Monthly GrabFood net revenue after fees and packaging rose 14% despite a lower total order count, because the channel was no longer being diluted by low-margin, high-complaint tail items.

Dine-in weekend revenue grew 11% over the same period after the social media and promotion budget was redirected toward the physical experience. The weekend family meal package contributed RM3,200 in its first month.

Rashdan's takeaway: "I thought more menu items meant more sales. The data showed me that more items just meant more problems — for the kitchen, for the ratings, and for the margin. We are doing less on GrabFood now, and making more from it." An AI assistant now helps him track delivery performance by item, flag menu items whose complaint rate is rising, and review weekly packaging costs before they become a problem again.
