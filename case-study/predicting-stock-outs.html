Predicting Stock-Outs Before They Happen

A minimarket chain owner managing three outlets in the Klang Valley was dealing with a familiar frustration: certain shelves kept running empty at the worst possible times. Customers would come in expecting to find daily essentials — instant noodles, cooking oil, canned goods — and leave empty-handed. He had been reordering stock whenever his total inventory level dropped below a fixed threshold. The problem kept happening anyway. He brought in Pau Analytics to find out why.

Pau Analytics reviewed 18 months of inventory movement records across all three outlets — more than 450 SKUs, tracking daily stock levels, reorder events, and stock-out occurrences. The analysis revealed something the owner had not expected: his total stock level was not the problem. The timing was.

What the Data Showed

Across 18 months, 73% of stock-out incidents were concentrated in the same 12 SKUs. These were everyday staples — instant noodles, rice (5kg and 10kg packs), cooking oil, canned sardines, sugar, and a handful of household cleaning items. Nothing exotic. Nothing seasonal. All of them fast-movers the owner believed were well-managed.

The second finding was more specific. These 12 SKUs ran out almost exclusively in the final three days before month-end — the same period when workers in the surrounding residential and industrial areas received their salaries. Demand on those three days was running 2.8 times the weekly average. The reorder system had been calibrated for steady demand. It had no mechanism for the demand surge.

Pau Analytics also found that the problem was not evenly distributed across the three outlets. One outlet, located near a workers' hostel cluster, accounted for 58% of all stock-out incidents on those 12 SKUs. The other two outlets showed the same pattern but at lower intensity. A uniform reorder policy was spreading buffer stock too thin where it was needed most.

What Changed

Pau Analytics recommended a two-part adjustment. The first was a tiered reorder schedule: weekly restocking for the 12 high-velocity SKUs, rather than the flat fortnightly cycle applied to the wider range. This brought more stock in more frequently, reducing the gap between the last reorder and the month-end peak.

The second change was outlet-specific buffer allocation. The outlet near the workers' hostel cluster received a standing order of 1.4x the normal buffer quantity for the 12 critical SKUs during the final week of each month. The other two outlets maintained their standard levels. This avoided over-stocking across the chain while targeting the location where the problem was concentrated.

The owner did not need to add products, renegotiate supplier terms, or hire more staff. The change was in the schedule and allocation logic — both of which were adjustable with the existing ordering system.

The Result

Over the three months following the adjustment, stock-out incidents on the 12 affected SKUs fell 64%. The outlet near the hostel cluster, which had been the main source of complaints, had zero stock-outs on those items in month three. The owner now tracks stock velocity by SKU and by outlet on a live dashboard. When a fast-mover shows unusual depletion midweek — a sign that month-end demand may be arriving early — the system flags it before the shelf goes empty. An AI assistant supports the owner in reviewing inventory trends and deciding where to adjust reorder quantities before problems occur.
