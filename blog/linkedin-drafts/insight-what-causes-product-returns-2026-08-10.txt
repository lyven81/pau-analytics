We spent a week investigating why a Malaysian e-commerce retailer's products were being returned. Three of our four main suspects came back clean.

The warehouse manager suspected star ratings. Low-rated items, he assumed, must come back more often. They didn't. Products with a 5-star average were returned at the same rate as products with a 3-star average.

He suspected review volume. Heavily-reviewed items, surely, were better understood by buyers. No link.

He suspected stockouts. Items that had been unavailable might have pushed customers to poor substitutes. Also cleared.

Two weak signals did turn up. Products sold at steeper discounts came back slightly more often. Higher-priced items had a marginally higher return rate. Both were real but not strong enough to build a fix around.

The most important finding: none of the data the warehouse was already collecting could reliably predict returns. The reason was simple — return reasons weren't being recorded. "Changed my mind", "damaged on arrival", "not as described" — all invisible.

The recommendation wasn't a policy change. It was a data collection change: log the return reason every time an item comes back. That single field, combined with delivery records and complaint history, is where the real answer will eventually come from.

Sometimes the right outcome of an analysis is knowing which questions you haven't been asking.

Check whether your returns data captures the reason — or just the event.
