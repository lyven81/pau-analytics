Is Your Pricing Right? What Data From a Malaysian Café Chain Reveals

Published 7 September 2026

Most business owners set prices once — when they first open — and then adjust only when something forces them to. A competitor undercuts them. A supplier raises costs. A customer pushes back. Pricing becomes reactive, driven by pressure rather than data.

But customers reveal whether your prices are right every day, through their buying behaviour. The question is whether you are reading the signal. A café chain owner in Kuala Lumpur discovered that reading it changed her average transaction value by nearly 10% — without running a single promotion or adding a new item to the menu.

The Problem With "Market Rate" Pricing

She ran three outlets for four years. Her prices matched the competition — she verified this every six months. Business was stable, but profit margins were thinner than her revenue justified. She assumed her prices were correct because customers were paying them without complaint.

Pau Analytics reviewed 14,800 transactions across her three outlets over 12 months. What the data showed was that prices being paid is not the same as prices being optimal. Two very different things were happening simultaneously on the same menu, and she had no way of seeing either without looking at the numbers.

What the Data Showed

The analysis examined each menu item for price elasticity — how much customer demand changed in response to price movement — and for its actual profit contribution at different price levels. The menu split clearly into two groups.

Group one: items customers were not sensitive to. Thirty-eight percent of the menu showed very low price sensitivity. Customer order volume barely moved when prices changed. The RM8.50 white coffee and RM9.00 teh tarik were pulling the same number of orders regardless of price. A modelled 15% increase on both items showed minimal impact on units sold. She was leaving measurable profit on the table every single day, and the money was simply not being collected.

Group two: items past the resistance point. Her RM16.90 dessert platter — the signature item — had lost 31% of its orders compared to a six-month period when it was priced at RM14.90. The extra RM2.00 on the ticket was costing more in lost volume than it gained in margin per unit. She had held the higher price because revenue per sale looked better on paper. The total profit contribution had actually declined.

Market-rate pricing tells you what everyone else is charging. Your data tells you what your customers are willing to pay. They are not the same number.

Three Decisions the Data Made Clear

The analysis produced three specific actions, each backed by the transaction record:

  • Raise the white coffee and teh tarik by RM1.00 each. Customers had already shown through their ordering behaviour that demand at this level was price-inelastic. The price increase would flow directly to margin.
  • Bring the dessert platter back to RM14.90. The volume lost at RM16.90 outweighed the gain per unit. Recovering volume at the lower price would increase total profit contribution.
  • Introduce a weekday afternoon bundle from 2pm to 5pm. Transaction data showed this was consistently the quietest window across all three outlets. A modest bundle discount during this window would convert near-zero revenue hours into a positive contributor without affecting peak-hour pricing.

None of these decisions required a guess. Each came directly from patterns already sitting in the point-of-sale data.

What Changed

The owner implemented all three changes and tracked the results for two months. Average transaction value across the three outlets increased by RM1.80 per bill — a 9.4% improvement. The dessert platter recovered to its previous order volume within four weeks of returning to RM14.90. The afternoon bundle created a revenue window that had been almost entirely empty before.

No new products were launched. No promotions were needed. No additional headcount was required. The improvement came entirely from pricing decisions that the data had been signalling for months — decisions that looked obvious in retrospect but were invisible without analysis.

What This Means for Your Business

If you have not looked at price elasticity for your top-selling items, you likely have at least one item that is underpriced and one that is sitting past customer resistance. Both are costing you money. The data to find them is already in your transaction records — it just has not been read from this angle.

Three questions worth asking about your own business:

  • Which items sell at the same volume regardless of whether you run a promotion? Those are candidates for a price increase.
  • Which items dropped in orders when you last raised the price — and never recovered? Those may be past their resistance point.
  • Which time windows in your day or week are consistently low-revenue? Those may respond to a bundled or time-limited offer that does not affect your main pricing.
