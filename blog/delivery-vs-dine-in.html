Delivery vs Dine-In: What Revenue Data From a Malaysian F&B Business Reveals

Published 2 November 2026

Every Malaysian F&B owner faces the same decision: how much of your business should be on GrabFood or foodpanda, and how much should stay at your tables? Delivery looks compelling because the average order value is higher. Customers ordering from home tend to add more items. You also save on the overhead of serving them — no waiting staff, no table turnover anxiety.

But there is a version of this story that does not end well. And it starts with a number that looks good until you subtract everything attached to it.

The Delivery Channel Looked Better Than It Was

A nasi lemak restaurant in Petaling Jaya listed on GrabFood in early 2023. Within six months, delivery was growing fast. The owner's GrabFood average order value was RM43, versus RM31 at his counter. He assumed that growth was profit.

Twelve months later, he asked Pau Analytics to look at his full channel picture. We reviewed 18,400 orders across the year — 61% dine-in, 30% GrabFood, 9% WhatsApp bulk orders for office deliveries.

The first finding confirmed what he already knew. GrabFood orders were 39% higher in average order value than dine-in.

The second finding changed everything. After GrabFood's platform commission of 30%, the net revenue per GrabFood transaction fell to RM30.10 — already below his RM31 dine-in average. When we added packaging costs — averaging RM4.80 per delivery order across containers, bags, and insulation for soups and curries — GrabFood net revenue per transaction dropped to RM25.30.

For every RM100 of GrabFood revenue showing on his books, he was keeping RM59.

The Menu Was the Problem

He had listed 34 items on GrabFood. We looked at which ones were actually ordered. Six items — nasi lemak standard set, nasi lemak ayam berempah, nasi lemak daging rendang, mee goreng mamak, fried chicken rice, and economy nasi campur — accounted for 72% of all delivery orders.

The remaining 28 items on the menu contributed 28% of orders. They also contributed 67% of all 1-star and 2-star reviews. These were dishes with longer prep times, complicated packaging, or presentations that fell apart in a delivery bag. Customers expected the same plate they would get at the counter. They got something that had spent 20 minutes bouncing around on a motorcycle.

The long menu was not just unprofitable. It was actively damaging his reputation on the platform while adding cost and kitchen complexity that was slowing down service for his dine-in customers.

More menu items did not mean more sales. They meant more complaints, more packaging cost, and a kitchen that was trying to do too many things at once.

The Timing Pattern Nobody Had Noticed

The data also showed where GrabFood orders were actually coming from — in time, not just location. 74% of all GrabFood orders arrived Monday through Friday between 11am and 1:30pm, driven by office buildings within a few kilometres of the restaurant. On weekends, GrabFood volume fell 61% below the weekday lunch average. Dine-in, meanwhile, peaked on weekends.

He had been running GrabFood promotions seven days a week, including weekend deals that were generating almost no orders. At the same time, he had no specific push for dine-in on weekends — the days when his tables were most likely to fill and his margins were cleanest.

Four Changes, One Better Quarter

Pau Analytics recommended four adjustments:

  • Streamline the GrabFood menu from 34 to 12 items — the six proven bestsellers plus six others selected for travel durability and simple packaging.
  • Set a RM20 minimum for WhatsApp bulk orders to cover delivery overhead on small runs. This slightly reduced order count but raised average WhatsApp order value from RM37 to RM44.
  • Run GrabFood promotions Monday to Friday only, during the 11am–1:30pm lunch window.
  • Redirect weekend social media spend to dine-in: photos of the physical space, a weekend family meal package, specials only available at the counter.

In the three months after these changes, packaging cost per delivery order fell from RM4.80 to RM2.90. GrabFood's average rating improved from 4.1 to 4.6 stars. Kitchen errors on delivery orders fell 61%. Monthly GrabFood net revenue — after fees and packaging — rose 14% despite fewer total orders on the platform.

Dine-in weekend revenue grew 11% after the marketing spend shifted. The family meal package generated RM3,200 in its first month.

What This Means for Your Business

If you are running both delivery and dine-in, the question worth asking is not "which channel has the higher order value?" The question is "which channel am I actually keeping revenue from?"

Platform fees, packaging costs, and the hidden kitchen overhead of managing a long delivery menu all eat into the gross number. The channel that looks strongest in your GrabFood dashboard may be the one costing you most to run.
