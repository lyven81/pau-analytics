What Your Repeat Customers Have in Common

Published 19 October 2026

Most business owners, when asked who their best customers are, describe them the same way: they spend a lot, they come often, and they have been coming for years. But when you look at the data, the origin story of those loyal customers is almost always the same. They gave you clear signals on the very first visit. You just were not collecting the right data to see them.

The Question Behind the Numbers

Amelia runs a specialty cake and pastry chain with three outlets in the Klang Valley. Business looked healthy on the surface — consistent footfall, good reviews, steady revenue. But when we looked at 16 months of transaction data — 5,200 unique customers and 11,800 purchases — she asked a question her POS system could not directly answer.

"Why do some customers keep coming back, and others don't?"

She assumed the answer had something to do with the product range or pricing. The data pointed somewhere else entirely.

The 14% Driving 51% of Revenue

The first thing we measured was how many visits each customer made over the study period. The result was uneven, as it almost always is.

The top segment — customers who visited four or more times over 16 months — represented just 14% of her total customer base. But they generated 51% of her total revenue.

This is not unusual in retail or F&B. But here is what often surprises owners: the average basket size of these high-frequency customers was only 12% higher than a one-time buyer. They were not spending dramatically more per visit. They were simply showing up more often.

Loyalty, in Amelia's case, was built on frequency — not on transaction size. Which meant the question worth asking was not "how do I get customers to spend more?" It was "how do I get the right customers to come back sooner?"

What the First Visit Reveals

The more useful question was not who her loyal customers were now — it was what they had looked like on day one.

We found that the clearest predictor of a second visit was the first-visit purchase pattern, not the amount spent. Customers who bought only one item on their first visit — even if it was a RM120 celebration cake — returned within 45 days at a rate of 19%. Customers who bought two or more items — a cake plus a coffee, or a pastry plus a drink — returned at a rate of 44%.

The spend mattered less than the mix. A two-item purchase suggested the customer was already treating the bakery as a regular stop, not a one-off event. They were browsing with habit in mind, not just buying for an occasion.

A celebration cake buyer shows up when they have a reason. An everyday buyer comes back because your shop is part of their routine. The first visit tells you which one you are dealing with.

Weekday Visitors vs. Weekend Visitors

The timing of the first visit also mattered more than Amelia expected.

Customers who first visited on a weekday (Monday to Friday) had a 41% return rate at the 30-day mark. Customers whose first visit fell on a weekend had a 24% return rate.

Her weekend footfall was twice her weekday footfall. But it was producing fewer loyal customers per visit.

The interpretation: weekend visitors often come because a market, event, or family outing has brought them nearby. Weekday visitors come with a destination in mind — they know what they want. Purpose-driven buyers build habits more readily than browsing-driven ones.

This does not mean weekend traffic is not valuable. It means the follow-up strategy for a first-time weekend visitor needs to work harder to create a second visit than it does for a first-time weekday visitor.

What Amelia Changed

Once she understood the pattern, Amelia made two targeted adjustments.

The first was operational. She introduced a "today's pairing" display near the register — a small card suggesting which drink or pastry goes well with that day's featured cake slice. The goal was to shift more first-time visitors from a single-item purchase to a two-item purchase. No promotion, no discount. Just a cue. Average transaction value increased by RM11 per visit. First-visit-to-second-visit conversion improved from 31% to 38% over three months.

The second was a simple communication habit. Each week, the team sent a short, personal-sounding WhatsApp message — "Your favourite almond croissant is fresh this Tuesday" — to any customer in her high-frequency segment who hadn't visited in 14 days. No discount. Just a reminder that felt personal rather than promotional.

The high-frequency segment grew from 14% to 19% of her customer base over six months.

What This Means for Your Business

The customers who will make up your loyal base already showed you what they were on the very first visit. The pattern is usually in your data: what they bought, how many items they picked, when they came in, and whether they came back within the first few weeks.

If you run a business with repeat-purchase potential — F&B, retail, wellness, services — these patterns are worth finding. Not to predict the future with certainty, but to stop treating all first-time buyers the same and to direct your retention effort toward the customers most likely to become habits.

Do you know what your first-visit data looks like? If you have transaction records going back six months or more, the answer is already there.
